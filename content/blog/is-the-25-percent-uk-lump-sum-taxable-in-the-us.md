If you are a US citizen or Green Card holder in the UK, the 25% tax-free lump sum is the question we are asked about most, and the one with the least satisfying answer. In the UK the treatment is settled. On the US side it is not, and the disagreement is real rather than a matter of one adviser being careless.

This article sets out what the treaty text actually says, the two positions practitioners take, and the points worth weighing before you draw the money.

## The short answer

**The conservative position is that the 25% UK Pension Commencement Lump Sum is taxable in the US, and it is the position most cross-border practitioners apply. A treaty-based argument for exemption exists and is not frivolous: it rests on Article 17(1)(b), which the saving clause expressly does not override. Whether that paragraph or Article 17(2) governs a PCLS is the unresolved question, and the IRS has published nothing dealing with it directly. This is a decision to take advice on before the payment is made.**

## What the treaty text actually says

Three provisions of the 2001 US-UK income tax convention interact here, and it is worth reading them in order.

**Article 1(4), the saving clause.** It applies "notwithstanding any provision of this Convention except paragraph 5 of this Article", and it lets each country tax its residents, and in the US case its citizens, as though the treaty had not entered into force.

**Article 1(5), the exceptions.** This is the list of provisions the saving clause does not touch. Among them, at sub-paragraph (a), are "sub-paragraph b) of paragraph 1 and paragraphs 3 and 5 of Article 17".

**Article 17.** Paragraph 1(b) deals with the amount of a pension or similar remuneration paid from a scheme established in the other country. Paragraph 2 deals with "a lump-sum payment derived from a pension scheme established in a Contracting State".

Read together, the position is narrower than it is often described. Article 17(1)(b) **is** carved out of the saving clause. Article 17(2) **is not**. So the argument is not really about whether the saving clause has exceptions. It is about which paragraph a Pension Commencement Lump Sum falls under.

## Position 1: the lump sum is US-taxable

This is the cautious default and the position most practitioners apply.

The reasoning treats the PCLS as a lump-sum payment within Article 17(2). Because Article 17(2) is absent from the Article 1(5) list, the saving clause operates and the US may tax its citizen on the payment, notwithstanding that the UK does not. Under US domestic law the distribution is then ordinary income in the year of receipt.

The document usually cited for this is **IRS Chief Counsel Memorandum AM2008-009** of 21 August 2008. Its subject is UK pension rollovers rather than the PCLS, and by its own terms it "may not be used or cited as precedent". Its analysis does, however, run the same route: it notes that Article 17(2) gives exclusive taxing rights to the state where the scheme sits, that the Article 1(5) exceptions do not cover Article 17(2), and that the US may therefore tax a lump sum paid from a UK scheme to a US resident.

HMRC's own manual reaches the same mechanical conclusion from the UK side. INTM163160 says Article 17(2) is "in effect overridden" by Article 1(4), so the country of residence may also tax the payment, with double-tax relief given in the usual way.

Neither source addresses the 25% PCLS specifically. Both support the mechanics the conservative position relies on.

## Position 2: the lump sum may be treaty-exempt

The competing argument characterises the PCLS not as an Article 17(2) lump-sum payment but as an amount falling within Article 17(1)(b), which concerns pension amounts paid from a scheme established in the other country. On that reading the exemption the UK gives can carry across, because Article 17(1)(b) sits on the Article 1(5) list and the saving clause does not reach it.

This is a minority position and it is less conservative, but it is a reading of the treaty text rather than wishful thinking. If you take it, you would generally disclose the treaty position on **Form 8833**. The penalty for failing to disclose a reportable treaty position is 1,000 US dollars for an individual under section 6712, and the position itself can be examined.

## Where the disagreement actually sits

It helps to be precise about what is and is not in dispute.

Not in dispute: the lump sum is tax-free in the UK; the US taxes its citizens on worldwide income; the treaty contains a saving clause; Article 17(1)(b) is excepted from it and Article 17(2) is not.

In dispute: which of those two paragraphs characterises a Pension Commencement Lump Sum.

That characterisation question is unresolved. The IRS has published no guidance addressing it directly, there is no decided case we are aware of that settles it, and the treaty does not define the boundary between the two paragraphs for this payment. Anyone who tells you the answer is obvious, in either direction, is overstating the position. **We flag this specific question as one for professional review on your facts rather than one to settle from general reading.**

## What this means if you are approaching retirement

If a PCLS is on your horizon, the decision has real financial consequences and should be made before the payment is taken, not after. The points that usually matter:

- the size of the lump sum relative to your wider US position
- the tax year the payment falls into, and what other income sits alongside it
- whether you hold Foreign Tax Credit carryforwards that could absorb a US liability
- your appetite for examination risk, which is a personal judgement rather than a technical one
- your filing history, including whether you already take treaty positions

If the UK withholds no tax, and on the tax-free portion it generally does not, there may be no foreign tax credit arising from the payment itself to offset a US charge. That is what surprises people: the money arrives whole, and the US bill arrives later with nothing attached to cover it.

## Reporting obligations do not go away

Whichever position you take, a treaty position changes how income is taxed. It does not remove reporting. Depending on the arrangement and your balances, the pension may still need to be considered for [FBAR](/services/us-expat-tax/fbar-filing) and [Form 8938](/resources/glossary/form-8938), and the distribution still belongs on the return even where you treat it as exempt. Treating a treaty position as a reporting exemption is a common and expensive error.

## Common mistakes we see

- Reading one confident article and assuming the question is settled. It is not, which is the point of this one.
- Taking the lump sum first and discovering the US position afterwards, when the options have narrowed.
- Relying on the treaty exemption without filing Form 8833, which is both a disclosure failure and a weaker position on examination.
- Ignoring state tax. Even after leaving the US, your state position at the time of withdrawal can matter.
- Assuming the Foreign Tax Credit will cover it. Where the UK charged no tax, often there is nothing to credit.
